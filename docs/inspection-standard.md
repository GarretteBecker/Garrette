# What the best inspectors look for — and where HomeKeeper goes past them

Research for B&M HomeKeeper, September 2026. Written for Garrette.

⚠️ **Read this the way you'd read a trade magazine, not a code book.** It's
research, not a licensed opinion. You're the licensed contractor
(PA #154223) — anything here you wouldn't say to a customer yourself,
strike out. Sources are at the bottom.

---

## 1. The short version

A home inspector and a HomeKeeper tech are doing **two different jobs**, and
the gap between them is your whole product.

|  | Home inspector | HomeKeeper tech |
|---|---|---|
| When | Once, during a sale | Four times a year, for years |
| What they're paid for | A snapshot of today | Whether it's getting worse |
| Allowed to operate things? | Largely no | Yes |
| Allowed to take things apart? | No — "non-invasive, visual" | Yes |
| Knows the house's history | No | **Yes — that's the Home Record** |
| Liability if they miss it | Their E&O policy | Your relationship |

An inspector's Standard of Practice is a **ceiling**, written by their trade
association to limit what they can be sued for. It is explicitly a
"non-invasive, visual examination of the accessible areas." Everything the
SoP *excludes* is a thing a homeowner still needs done, and nobody is doing
it for them.

**That list of exclusions is your service.**

## 2. What Pennsylvania actually requires

PA's **Home Inspection Law (Act 114 of 2000, 68 Pa.C.S. ch. 75)** doesn't
write its own technical standard. It does four things:

1. The inspector must be a member in good standing of a national association
   (ASHI, InterNACHI, NAHI) and **follow that association's Standard of
   Practice**
2. Continuing education
3. Reports consistent with that standard
4. E&O and general liability, **not less than $100,000 per occurrence and
   $500,000 aggregate**

So "the PA standard" is in practice **the ASHI / InterNACHI SoP**. That
covers: roof, exterior, foundation and structure, attic and insulation,
interior, HVAC, plumbing, electrical.

### What that standard explicitly does NOT cover

This is the important half. A general inspection does **not** include:

- Radon, mold, asbestos, lead, or any environmental testing
- Water quality or well yield
- Septic tanks or drain fields
- Anything underground — sewer laterals, buried oil tanks, irrigation
- Anything concealed — behind drywall, under floors, inside walls
- Any **measurement of performance** — nobody records a temperature split,
  a CO reading in ppm, or an amp draw
- Any **trend** — there's no "last year this was 17°, this year it's 11°"
- Chimney interiors, beyond the visible exterior
- Estimating remaining life, or what anything costs to fix

## 3. What PA specifically makes matter

Pennsylvania's **Seller Disclosure Law (68 Pa.C.S. § 7304)** is the best
single list of what this state considers material about a house. Sellers
must disclose on: roof; basements and crawl spaces; termites and wood
destroying insects; water and sewage; structural problems; additions and
structural changes; plumbing; heating and A/C; electrical; other equipment
and appliances; hazardous substances; storm water facilities; and —
distinctively — **"soils, drainage, boundaries and sinkholes."**

Five PA-specific things sit on top of the national picture:

### Radon — and this is Lancaster County's headline

Pennsylvania has roughly the **third-highest average indoor radon in the
country**, and Lancaster County is among the worst in Pennsylvania:

- County average indoor radon: **11.4 pCi/L**
- National average: **1.3 pCi/L**
- **64%** of Lancaster County tests between 1986 and 2016 came back above
  the EPA's 4.0 pCi/L action level — over 20,000 homes

A test kit is about $20. **Most of your members have never tested, and the
ones with a mitigation system have never checked whether the fan still
runs.** That fan is a consumable — it's a motor running 24/7 — and when it
dies, nothing beeps. This is the single highest-value, lowest-cost thing on
your whole list.

### Karst limestone and sinkholes

Much of Lancaster County sits on limestone that dissolves under acidic
groundwater. It's the reason "sinkholes" is a named disclosure category in
PA law. What it means practically: **water management around the foundation
isn't cosmetic here.** Downspouts discharging at the foundation, negative
grade, a leaking buried line — in karst, those don't just wet a basement,
they wash out fines under the footing. Slow subsidence shows up as stair-
step cracks and doors that stop latching.

### Stucco — real, but check whose county

The "stucco crisis" is heavily concentrated in **Chester, Delaware and
Montgomery** counties — homes built roughly 1985–2005 with stucco or EIFS
over no drainage plane, no weep screed, and no window flashing. Remediation
runs $25k–$300k. Two honest caveats: the "80% of stucco homes have failed"
figure comes from **remediation contractors**, who sell the fix, and the
concentration is SE PA rather than Lancaster specifically. But if a member
has hard-coat stucco from that era, the failure mode is real, it's invisible
from outside, and the tell is at **window and door corners, kickouts, and
band joists** — which is exactly where you should be looking anyway.

### Private wells — nobody is checking these

**PA does not regulate private water wells at all.** No mandated testing, no
construction standard for most of the state's history. In an agricultural
county, that matters: nitrates from fertilizer and manure, and coliform from
septic. EPA recommends **annual** coliform bacteria and nitrate testing, and
almost nobody does it. If you serve well homes, an annual test — through a
**PA-accredited lab** — is a service they can't get anywhere else on a
schedule.

### Old housing stock

PA has some of the oldest housing in the country, and four specific things
now get homes **surcharged, non-renewed, or refused** by insurers:

| What | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| **Knob and tube** | No ground path. Damaged wire or overload turns into heat rather than a tripped breaker |
| **Aluminum branch circuits** (~1965–73) | Expansion/contraction loosens terminations; loose connection = heat = fire |
| **Federal Pacific Stab-Lok / Zinsco panels** | Breakers that don't trip. See below |
| **Polybutylene supply** (~1978–95) | Fails at fittings, behind drywall, at full pressure with no warning |

On **Federal Pacific**: CPSC-funded testing reported Stab-Lok double-pole
breakers failing to trip in a large share of overload tests, and a 2005 New
Jersey court found FPE had fraudulently labeled them as meeting safety
standards. Be straight with people about the history — **CPSC closed its
investigation in 1983 without ordering a recall**, and the engineering
debate has never fully settled. It doesn't matter much in practice:
**insurers act on it anyway**, and that's a real consequence for your member
regardless of who's right.

## 4. What separates the top inspectors

Four things, all of which you can do better than they can because you're
coming back:

1. **They measure instead of eyeballing.** A combustion analyzer, a
   thermometer, a clamp meter, a moisture meter. A number beats an opinion,
   and a number you can compare to last year beats both.
2. **Thermal imaging.** Finds what's behind the drywall — wet insulation,
   missing insulation, hot terminations in a panel, a blocked supply run.
3. **They go where the SoP stops.** Sewer scope, drone for a roof too steep
   to walk, borescope into a heat exchanger.
4. **They coordinate the rest.** Radon, well, termite, sewer — the client
   doesn't have to chase four vendors. *You already do this. It's your
   dispatch board.*

## 5. The deep preventative maintenance list

Organised by system. **Bold = already in the app's checklist draft.**
Everything not bold is new, and the reason it's here is in the right column.

### Heating, cooling and ventilation

| Check | Interval | Why it earns a slot |
|---|---|---|
| **Furnace combustion / heat exchanger** | Annual (fall) | CO kills 400+ a year in the US. A cracked exchanger is the classic cause |
| **Measure flue CO in ppm and record it** | Annual | Healthy is roughly 10–50 ppm in the flue and **0 in the house**. Past ~100 ppm is incomplete combustion. This is the number that turns an opinion into evidence |
| **Measure A/C temperature split and record it** | Annual (spring) | ~15–20°F on a healthy system. Year over year, a falling split is a charge or airflow problem months before the member notices |
| **Clamp the compressor amp draw** | Annual | Rising amps against the nameplate = a compressor heading out. Nobody catches this by looking |
| **Static pressure across the air handler** | Annual | High static kills blower motors and coils. It's the root cause behind half the "my system is weak" calls |
| **Condensate float switch — test it, don't look at it** | 2× a year | The device that stops a ceiling coming down |
| **Flue, vent connector and chimney liner** | Annual | Disconnected vent connectors and unlined masonry flues are a CO path |
| **Filter — change and record the size** | Every visit | Size on the Home Record so they can buy their own |
| **Register and return airflow, room by room** | Annual | |
| **Attic ventilation: soffits clear, ridge working** | Annual (summer) | Insulation stuffed into soffits bakes the roof and doubles its ageing |
| **Refrigerant line insulation** | Annual | Missing line-set insulation is free efficiency, permanently lost |
| **Thermostat schedule and batteries** | Annual | |

### Plumbing

| Check | Interval | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Water heater age against its serial** | Annual | **Average age at failure: 10.7 years. By 12, nearly 3 in 4 have failed.** Average insurance cost: **$4,444**. This is the most predictable expensive failure in a house, and it's on the Home Record already |
| **Anode rod** | Every 2–3 years | The single thing that decides whether a heater makes 8 years or 15 |
| **T&P valve, and the discharge pipe terminates correctly** | Annual | |
| **Sediment flush** | Annual | |
| **Expansion tank charge** | Annual | A dead expansion tank on a closed system spikes pressure and pops the T&P |
| **Exercise the main shutoff** | Annual | A valve that never moves seizes. A seized valve snaps when it's finally needed — that's the difference between a leak and a flood |
| **Exercise every angle stop under sinks and toilets** | Annual | Same logic, and these are the ones people actually reach for |
| Whole-house static water pressure, with a gauge | Annual | Over 80 psi destroys everything downstream and voids some warranties. A $12 gauge and a PRV finding pays for the visit |
| **Washing machine hoses — age, bulging, cracking** | Annual | Rubber is a 5-year part. Braided stainless is $20 |
| **Sump pump: float, check valve, discharge** | 2× a year | |
| Sump backup — battery or water-powered, and test it | Annual | A sump with no backup stops exactly when the storm knocks the power out |
| **Under every sink, by hand not by eye** | Every visit | A slow leak is dry by the time you look at it |
| **Toilets and faucets for silent leaks (dye tablet)** | Every visit | |
| Supply piping material identified and recorded | Once | Polybutylene changes their insurance and their risk |
| **Outside spigots and vacuum breakers** | Spring | |
| **Hoses off before the freeze** | Fall | A hose left on a frost-free sillcock defeats it entirely — the commonest burst pipe there is |
| Main drain / lateral — scope it once | Once, then as needed | Cast iron and clay laterals in older PA towns. A root intrusion found on camera is a $400 job; found by backup it's a $12,000 one |

### Life safety

| Check | Interval | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Test smoke and CO alarms** | Every visit | |
| **Read the date stamp — they expire** | Annual | 10 years for smoke, 7–10 for CO. **An expired alarm still beeps when you test it.** That's the whole trap |
| Coverage check — one per bedroom, one per level, CO near sleeping areas | Annual | Most houses are under-covered, and nobody has ever counted |
| **Fire extinguisher charge, date, location** | Annual | |
| **Dryer vent — clean the whole run, check the outside flap** | Annual | **Failure to clean causes 33% of dryer fires, and those fires cause half the dryer fire deaths.** ~$200M in property damage a year |
| **Garage door auto-reverse on a 2x4, and the photo eyes** | Annual | Two minutes. It's the one that matters |
| Handrails, guards and baluster spacing | Annual | |
| Gas appliance connectors — no flexible connector through a wall | Annual | |

### Envelope, roof and water management

| Check | Interval | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Roof: shingles, flashing, boots, ridge, penetrations** | 2× a year | Pipe boots are the commonest roof leak and the cheapest fix |
| **Gutters, downspouts, and where they discharge** | 2× a year | In karst, discharge location isn't cosmetic |
| **Grading and drainage away from the foundation** | 2× a year | |
| **Ice dam evidence** | Winter | The cause is attic heat loss, not the gutters |
| **Siding, trim, caulk, paint** | 2× a year | |
| Stucco/EIFS at window corners, kickouts, band joists | Annual, if applicable | The failure is invisible from outside |
| **Deck: probe posts at grade, ledger flashing, fasteners** | Annual | Probe, don't look. Ledger attachment is what actually collapses decks |
| **Seal penetrations before the cold** | Fall | |
| Window and door operation, locks, failed seals | Annual | |

### Structure, basement and ground

| Check | Interval | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Basement and crawl moisture, vapour barrier** | Every visit | |
| Foundation crack map — **photograph and measure the same cracks each time** | Annual | A crack is meaningless. A crack that grew 3mm in a year is a finding. This is something no inspector can ever do, and you can |
| Sill plate, band joist, and anywhere wood meets ground | Annual | |
| **Wood destroying insects — mud tubes, bee holes, rot** | Annual | Named in PA's disclosure law |

| **Attic: insulation depth, ventilation, daylight, stains** | Annual | |

### Environmental — the part inspectors are not allowed to touch

| Check | Interval | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Radon: has it ever been tested?** | Once, then every 2 years | Lancaster averages 11.4 pCi/L against 1.3 nationally |
| **Radon mitigation fan running and manometer reading correct** | Every visit | It's a motor running 24/7 and **nothing alerts you when it dies** |
| Well: coliform bacteria and nitrates, PA-accredited lab | Annual | PA regulates nothing here. Agricultural county |
| Well: pressure tank charge, pump short-cycling | Annual | |
| Septic: years since pump-out, drain field, surfacing | Annual | Most tanks want it every 3–5 years |
| Buried oil tank — identified and recorded | Once | A leaking buried tank is an environmental liability that follows the property |
| Indoor humidity reading, recorded | Every visit | 30–50%. Above 60% you're growing something |
| Dehumidifier operation and drain | Summer | |

## 6. The one thing that makes this yours

Everything above, any good inspector could look at once.

**What none of them can do is compare it to last year.** You can, because
you've got the Home Record and you're coming back in ninety days.

So the highest-value change to your checklists isn't adding more items —
it's making a handful of them **record a number instead of a pass/fail**:

- A/C temperature split, in °F
- Flue CO, in ppm
- Compressor amp draw, against the nameplate
- Static pressure across the air handler
- Water pressure at the hose bib, in psi
- Indoor humidity, in %
- Radon manometer reading
- Foundation crack width, at the same three marked spots

Eight numbers. After a year they're data; after three years they're the
reason a member never leaves, because the report says *"your split has gone
from 18° to 13° over two years, here's what that means and here's what it
costs to deal with before it quits in July."*

No inspector in Pennsylvania can write that sentence. You can.
